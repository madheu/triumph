# Praxis 5004 Social Studies Study Guide: A High-Yield Review Plan

*2026-08-20 · Reading time: about 9 min*

The Praxis Elementary Education: Social Studies Subtest (5004) covers a wide span of history and social science—but your preparation does not need to become an endless list of names and dates. The best approach is to learn a small number of organizing frameworks, practice applying them, and spend the most time where the test places the most weight.

As of August 20, 2026, ETS lists Praxis 5004 with **60 questions in 60 minutes** and an individual test fee of **$64**.[5] ETS’s detailed description calls the questions selected-response, although its feature panel also mentions numeric-entry; prepare primarily for selected-response items but review the live test page before registering. Confirm your state’s current test-code requirement as well. The published timing gives you an average of one minute per question, so both knowledge and decision speed matter.

## The High-Yield U.S. History Timeline

Do not memorize American history as disconnected facts. Build a cause-and-effect timeline around seven eras.

### 1. Colonization and the Revolution

Know why European powers established colonies, how regional economies differed, and why conflict with Britain intensified after the French and Indian War. Connect taxation and representation disputes to the Declaration of Independence, then distinguish the military turning points from the political principles of the Revolution.

**Anchor chain:** imperial rivalry → colonial taxation → resistance → independence → weak national government.

### 2. Founding and the Constitution

Focus on why the Articles of Confederation failed and how the Constitution addressed those weaknesses. Review:

- Federalism: authority divided between national and state governments
- Separation of powers: legislative, executive, and judicial branches
- Checks and balances: each branch limits the others
- Popular sovereignty: legitimate power comes from the people
- The Bill of Rights: protections against federal overreach

Be able to apply these ideas. For example, if a court invalidates a law because it conflicts with the Constitution, the tested principle is **judicial review**, not federalism.

### 3. Expansion, Reform, and Sectional Conflict

Connect westward expansion to Indigenous displacement, economic growth, and the expansion debate over slavery. Review major antebellum reform movements, including abolition, women’s rights, temperance, and public education.

**Anchor chain:** territorial growth → slavery debate → failed compromises → secession → Civil War.

### 4. Civil War and Reconstruction

Prioritize causes, wartime strategy at a broad level, emancipation, and constitutional change. Know the lasting importance of the Reconstruction Amendments:

- **13th Amendment:** ended slavery
- **14th Amendment:** citizenship, due process, and equal protection
- **15th Amendment:** prohibited denying voting rights on the basis of race

Then connect Reconstruction’s end to disenfranchisement and segregation.

### 5. Industrialization and the Progressive Era

Study how railroads, factories, immigration, urbanization, and corporate growth changed daily life. Pair each problem with a response: unsafe working conditions with labor organizing, political corruption with civil-service reform, and concentrated business power with antitrust action.

### 6. Depression, World Wars, and the Cold War

Review the causes and effects of U.S. participation in both world wars, the Great Depression, and the New Deal. For the Cold War, organize events around **containment**, alliances, proxy conflicts, the arms race, and competition between political-economic systems.

### 7. Civil Rights and Contemporary America

Connect constitutional protections to civil rights strategies such as litigation, boycotts, mass protest, and federal legislation. Review major changes in voting rights, immigration, technology, globalization, and the relationship between federal and state authority.

## Government and Citizenship: Learn the Decision Rules

Government questions often present a scenario rather than ask for a definition. Use these decision rules:

- **Who has the power?** Identify the level or branch of government.
- **What limits the power?** Look for a constitutional protection or another branch’s check.
- **What kind of participation is occurring?** Voting, petitioning, volunteering, contacting officials, and peaceful protest are forms of civic engagement.
- **Which source of law controls?** The Constitution is supreme; statutes, regulations, and court decisions operate within that framework.

Original practice example: A governor vetoes a state education bill, and the state legislature votes to enact it over the veto. This illustrates **checks and balances within state government**.

## Geography, Anthropology, and Sociology: Use the PLACE Framework

For the category worth about 30% of the test, remember **PLACE**:

- **P — Physical systems:** climate, landforms, water, ecosystems, and natural hazards
- **L — Location and maps:** absolute versus relative location, scale, direction, and spatial patterns
- **A — Adaptation:** how people alter or adjust to environments
- **C — Culture and communities:** beliefs, institutions, language, migration, and social groups
- **E — Exchange:** movement of people, products, ideas, and technology

Practice reading maps, charts, population pyramids, and short demographic descriptions. Ask what pattern the evidence supports before looking for a familiar term.

Original practice example: A city expands rail service along a densely populated corridor, and new housing clusters near stations. The clearest geographic concept is the relationship between **transportation networks and settlement patterns**.

For anthropology and sociology, distinguish the scale of analysis. Anthropology commonly examines culture and human development across societies; sociology emphasizes social relationships, groups, and institutions. Both may address norms, identity, family, religion, or social change.

## World History: Study Turning Points, Not Every Dynasty

Build a compact world-history framework around six developments:

1. **Early civilizations:** agriculture, cities, writing, government, and social hierarchy
2. **Classical societies:** political ideas, trade, philosophy, law, and empire
3. **Major belief systems:** origins, core ideas, diffusion, and cultural influence
4. **Regional exchange:** Silk Roads, Indian Ocean trade, trans-Saharan routes, and the spread of goods and ideas
5. **Global transformation:** Renaissance, Reformation, scientific change, exploration, and colonization
6. **Modern era:** revolutions, industrialization, imperialism, world wars, decolonization, and globalization

For each turning point, answer four questions: **What caused it? What changed? Who gained or lost power? What continued?** This structure is more useful than memorizing an isolated year.

## Economics: Master the Core Relationships

Prioritize concepts that can be tested through everyday situations:

- Scarcity forces individuals and societies to make choices.
- Opportunity cost is the best alternative given up.
- Supply and demand influence price and quantity.
- Competition and incentives shape producer and consumer behavior.
- Specialization and trade can increase total output.
- Inflation reduces purchasing power when prices rise faster than income.
- Fiscal policy involves government spending and taxation; monetary policy concerns the money supply and interest-rate conditions.

Original practice example: A school uses limited funds to replace buses instead of renovating its library. The renovation is the **opportunity cost** if it was the best rejected alternative.

## The One-Minute Pacing Plan

With 60 questions in 60 minutes, use a simple three-pass system:

### First 40 seconds: Solve

Read the question stem, identify the tested concept, and predict the answer before comparing choices when possible. Eliminate options contradicted by chronology, geography, constitutional structure, or the evidence provided.

### Next 10 seconds: Decide

If two choices remain, choose the one that most directly answers the exact question. Do not keep debating merely because another option is generally true.

### Final 10 seconds: Mark and Move

If you are still uncertain, make your best choice, mark the item if the testing interface permits, and move on. Protect later questions from one difficult item.

Use clock checkpoints to stay honest:

- Around question 15: about 45 minutes remaining
- Around question 30: about 30 minutes remaining
- Around question 45: about 15 minutes remaining
- Final minutes: return to marked items and confirm that every question has an answer

## Final Praxis 5004 Study Checklist

Before test day, make sure you can:

- Explain the seven U.S. history eras as cause-and-effect chains
- Apply federalism, checks and balances, and constitutional rights to scenarios
- Interpret maps, charts, migration patterns, and demographic evidence
- Compare major world-history turning points across regions and periods
- Solve basic scarcity, opportunity-cost, supply-and-demand, and policy questions
- Maintain an average pace of one question per minute
- Explain why each wrong practice answer is wrong
