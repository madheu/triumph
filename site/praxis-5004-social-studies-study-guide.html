Praxis 5004 Social Studies Study Guide: Timelines and Practice

2026-08-20 · Updated 2026-09-07 · About 15 min

The Praxis Elementary Education: Social Studies Subtest (5004) covers a wide span of history and social science. Your preparation does not need to become an endless list of names and dates — the better plan is to learn a small number of organizing frameworks, practice applying them to materials (maps, tables, short documents), and spend the most time where the test places the most weight.[1]

This guide covers test code 5004, not Elementary Education: Fundamentals — Social Studies (8004). The 8004 has different question counts, category weights, and timing; its specs are in its own companion and are not mixed into the 5004 plan below.[2] Confirm which code your state requires before you register.

Before you build a schedule, take Learndiag's free Praxis 5001 readiness diagnostic — it points your next study hour at the right content area instead of asking you to review everything equally.

ETS lists 5004 at 60 questions in 60 minutes.[1] That works out to one minute per question on average, but treat it as a target range, not a per-item deadline: a map-reading item can take twenty seconds, a document-analysis item two minutes. Practice finishing in about 55 minutes so you have about five minutes to check marked items — that 5-minute review buffer is a personal practice goal, not an ETS rule.

Reconstruction amendments — know the difference

AmendmentWhat it saidWhere it fell short
13th (1865)Abolished slavery (except as punishment for crime)Did not address the legal status and rights of newly freed people
14th (1868)Birthright citizenship; due process; equal protectionEnforcement depended on federal action that was withdrawn after 1877
15th (1870)Voting rights not denied on account of raceLeft room for poll taxes, literacy tests, and intimidation

Do not write the 15th as "immediately gave everyone the vote." It prohibited one specific basis of denial; states still used other barriers, which is why the civil rights movement had to press for the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

Government and Citizenship: Learn the Decision Rules

Government questions present a scenario more often than they ask for a definition. Four rules cover most of them:

  • Who has the power? Identify the level (federal/state/local) and the branch.
  • What limits the power? Look for a constitutional protection or another branch's check.
  • What kind of participation? Voting, petitioning, contacting officials, and peaceful protest are civic engagement; violence is not.
  • Which law controls? The Constitution is supreme; statutes, regulations, and court decisions operate inside it.

Worked example: A governor vetoes a state education bill, and the state legislature votes to enact it over the veto. Which principle does this illustrate?

Answer: checks and balances — one branch (executive) can block another (legislature), and the legislature can override with enough votes. The distractors, if they appear in the options, are:

  • Federalism — divides power between national and state governments. Nothing in the scenario involves the national government, so this is out.
  • Judicial review — a court striking down a law as unconstitutional. No court is involved here.
  • Popular sovereignty — political authority comes from the people. The scenario shows, meaningfully, that the question is about the relationship between two branches, not the source of authority.

Second worked example: A court rules that a state law violates the U.S. Constitution. What is the court exercising?

Answer: judicial review. This is also a form of checks and balances (the judiciary checking the legislature), so if both terms appear as options, the stem has to ask for the most specific mechanism. When you see a court invalidating a law, name judicial review; use "checks and balances" for the general limit one branch places on another. Read the stem to see which level of specificity it wants.

Geography, Anthropology, and Sociology

This category is worth about 30% of the test, and it rewards working with materials — maps, tables, population data — more than memorizing place names.[1]

PLACE is a Learndiag mnemonic, not an official ETS framework. It is a memory hook to organize what the official outline covers; use it as a checklist, not as a claim about how ETS names its categories:

  • P — Physical systems: climate, landforms, water, ecosystems, natural hazards
  • L — Location and maps: absolute vs relative location, scale, direction, spatial patterns — includes scale-map questions (e.g., reading a stated ratio such as 1:24,000 and converting a measured distance)
  • A — Adaptation: how people alter or adjust to environments
  • C — Culture and communities: beliefs, institutions, language, migration, social groups
  • E — Exchange: movement of people, products, ideas, technology

For anthropology and sociology, distinguish the scale of analysis: anthropology tends to study culture and human development across societies; sociology emphasizes social relationships, groups, and institutions. Both cover norms, identity, family, religion, and social change.

Material-based example (migration data): A table shows a state's population rising fastest in the 20–39 age group in the two decades after a major highway opens nearby. The best-supported claim is that transportation access is associated with migration into the region — not that the highway alone caused all growth, because the table shows association, not proof of cause.

Material-based example (scale map): On a map with scale 1 inch = 50 miles, a city pair is 3 inches apart on the page. The ground distance is 150 miles (3 × 50). Practice this before test day: scale problems are routine arithmetic once you read the ratio correctly.

Economics: Master the Core Relationships

Prioritize concepts testable through everyday situations:

  • Scarcity forces choices.
  • Opportunity cost is the best rejected alternative.
  • Supply and demand influence price and quantity, all else equal.
  • Competition and incentives shape behavior.
  • Specialization and trade can increase total output.
  • Fiscal policy = government spending and taxation; monetary policy = central bank control of money and interest rates.

Worked example (opportunity cost): A school uses limited funds to replace buses instead of renovating its library. The school's opportunity cost is the library renovation — on the assumption that the renovation was the next-best, feasible alternative use of the same funds. If the renovation was not feasible (the roof leaked and had to be fixed first, for example), it is not the opportunity cost; the concept only applies when a real alternative is given up.

Worked example (purchasing power): Your nominal income rises 3% in a year while prices rise 5%. Your purchasing power falls by about 2 percentage points — the 3% raise buys less than last year's income because prices grew faster. Items may phrase this as a real vs nominal income question; the math is the difference between the two percentages.

Worked example (supply and demand): A drought reduces the wheat harvest. Holding other factors constant, the wheat supply decreases, so the price rises. The "all else equal" clause matters: if demand also fell at the same time, the price effect is ambiguous, and a good item will say which side changed or ask what happens ceteris paribus.

Test-Day Pacing Strategy

Sixty questions in 60 minutes means the average is one minute per question, but items are not equal. Skip the rigid "40 seconds solve + 10 decide + 10 mark" split — it misleads you into hurrying easy items and starving hard ones. Instead:

  • Read the stem, identify the concept, and eliminate options contradicted by chronology, geography, constitutional structure, or the evidence provided.
  • If two choices remain, pick the one that most directly answers the exact question.
  • If you are still stuck, pick your best answer, mark it if the interface allows, and move on. Protect the rest of the test from one hard item.

Use checkpoints as a guide (not deadlines): around question 15, about 45 minutes left; question 30, about 30 minutes left; question 45, about 15 minutes left. Aim to finish with about 5 minutes to check marked items — again, a personal practice target, not an ETS requirement.

Final Praxis 5004 Study Checklist

Before test day, you should be able to do these (not just nod along):

  • Explain the seven U.S. history eras as cause-and-effect chains with the anchor dates above
  • Apply federalism, checks and balances, judicial review, and constitutional rights to a scenario — and name the most specific mechanism when asked
  • Read a scale map and convert measured distance to ground distance
  • Infer a point from migration or demographic data without overclaiming cause
  • Identify the opportunity cost only when a feasible next-best alternative is stated
  • Distinguish fiscal from monetary policy
  • Match a world-history turning point to its trigger, spread, and result
  • Finish a 60-question set in about 55 minutes, leaving a check buffer
  • Explain why every wrong practice answer is wrong

The highest-yield plan is not "review everything." It is diagnose, prioritize, apply, and retest. Start with Learndiag's free Praxis 5001 readiness diagnostic, then use your results to turn this guide into a focused schedule.
