# Praxis 5001 Study Guide: What's Actually on All Four Subtests

*Reading time: 6 min · Based on the official ETS Study Companion*

The Praxis 5001 — *Elementary Education: Multiple Subjects* — is not one exam. It is **four separate tests**, each scored independently, and you must pass all four to earn your license. Candidates who treat it as "one exam" are the ones who discover, too late, that they failed a single gate.

This guide breaks down the official structure of all four subtests — what's on them, how they're weighted, and how the scoring rules change the way you should study. It's built from the ETS Study Companion, not from guesswork.

> **The one fact that changes everything:** the 5001 scores each subtest separately. Score high on math and fail science — you don't pass. You retake *science only*, full price, 28 days later. Your study plan should be built around your weakest gate, not around "averaging out" across four subjects.

## The four gates, at a glance

| Subtest | Questions | Time | Formats |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `5002` Reading & Language Arts | 80 | 90 min | Selected-response |
| `5003` Mathematics | 50 | 65 min | Selected-response, numeric-entry |
| `5004` Social Studies | 60 | 60 min | Selected-response |
| `5005` Science | 55 | 60 min | Selected-response |

Total: 245 questions across 4 hours 35 minutes. Each subtest is timed and scored separately — there is no combined "total score" that saves you.

## 5002 · Reading & Language Arts *(80 questions, 90 minutes)*

This subtest assesses what an entry-level elementary teacher needs to teach reading and writing. It breaks into two content categories:

- **Reading — about 38 questions (47%)**: phonemic awareness and phonics, word recognition, vocabulary, reading comprehension, and the skills teachers use to develop them in children.
- **Writing, Speaking, and Listening — about 42 questions (53%)**: the writing process, conventions of standard English, and the instructional knowledge behind teaching writing and oral language.

What surprises candidates most: this is a *teaching* exam. You need to know the reading research — phoneme awareness, decoding, the difference between assessment and diagnosis — not just read well yourself.

## 5003 · Mathematics *(50 questions, 65 minutes)*

The math subtest is shorter than reading but covers a wide band, and it includes **numeric-entry questions** where you type an answer rather than choose it:

- **Numbers and Operations — 20 questions (40%)**: number sense, place value, fractions, decimals, percents, ratios, and operations.
- **Algebraic Thinking — 15 questions (30%)**: patterns, expressions, equations, functions, and proportional reasoning.
- **Geometry and Measurement, Data, Statistics, and Probability — 15 questions (30%)**: shapes, measurement, data displays, probability, and how to teach them.

Two things trip candidates up here. First, roughly a third of the items are **mathematics pedagogy** — "a student confuses 3/8 and 1/2, which representation helps them see the error?" — not computation. Second, many candidates haven't touched this content in years; the math is elementary-level, but fluency matters under a 65-minute clock.

## 5004 · Social Studies *(60 questions, 60 minutes)*

Sixty questions in sixty minutes — the tightest pace of the four. Content categories:

- **United States History, Government, and Citizenship — 27 questions (45%)**: the Constitution, branches of government, key eras and figures, citizenship.
- **Geography, Anthropology, and Sociology — 18 questions (30%)**: physical and human geography, culture, and how people interact with place.
- **World History and Economics — 15 questions (25%)**: major world civilizations, and basic economic concepts like opportunity cost and markets.

The breadth is the challenge: US history alone spans colonial era to today. Expect questions that ask about *teaching* these topics — like choosing an activity that helps students analyze why a city grew beside a river.

## 5005 · Science *(55 questions, 60 minutes)*

Science splits nearly evenly across three domains:

- **Earth Science — about 17–18 questions (~33%)**: the water cycle, weather, rocks and soil, space.
- **Life Science — about 18–19 questions (~33%)**: cells, organisms, ecosystems, plant and animal life.
- **Physical Science — about 18–19 questions (~33%)**: matter, energy, forces, motion, and simple machines.

Beyond content, expect **science inquiry and pedagogy** items — controlling variables in an investigation, designing a fair test, interpreting observations. Community reports consistently list science among the hardest gates, largely because the knowledge surface is wide and most adults haven't reviewed it in years.

## Scoring: the rule that reshapes your study plan

Each subtest produces its own scaled score, typically reported on a 100–200 scale. Passing lines **vary by state** and by subtest — commonly between 150 and 165, sometimes higher. What that means practically:

- You need four passing scores, not one good average.
- If you fail one subtest, you retake only that one — but you pay the full subtest fee and wait at least 28 days between attempts.
- Your **weakest gate determines whether you pass**. Studying all four equally is the single most common pattern among candidates who retake.

> **Reality check from the data:** national research (NCTQ) has found that a majority of first-time elementary teacher candidates do not pass the most widely used licensure exam, and first-time pass rates have been reported *below the bar exam*. This is a genuinely hard, high-stakes test — not a formality.

## How to use this structure (smarter, not harder)

1. **Diagnose first.** Before you touch a study guide, get a baseline: which of the four gates is already near passing, and which is far below? Candidates who spend ~60% of their time on the weakest gate pass more reliably than candidates who "cover everything."
2. **Learn the pedagogy items.** All four subtests mix content with teaching knowledge. Flash-card the terminology (phoneme, opportunity cost, controlled variable) — these are often the difference between a 158 and a 163.
3. **Practice under the clock.** 5004 gives you one minute per question. 5003 adds numeric entry. If you've never timed a full subtest, you'll feel it on test day.
4. **Know your state's passing line** and aim above it with margin — don't aim at the minimum.
