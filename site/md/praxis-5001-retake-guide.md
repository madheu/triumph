# Failed a Praxis 5001 Subtest? Retake Rules, Costs, and What to Do Next

*2026-09-07 · Reading time: about 4 min*

You opened your Praxis score report and saw it: **NOT PASSED** on one—or maybe two—of your Elementary Education: Multiple Subjects subtests. Maybe it was Reading and Language Arts (5002). Maybe math (5003). Maybe Science (5005) got you by two points.

Here is the truth: **failing a Praxis 5001 subtest is common.** The 5001 is a broad four-subject battery covering reading pedagogy, mathematics, social studies, and science. Most elementary teachers have natural strength in two or three areas and gaps in the others. A subtest failure is a data point about preparation, not a verdict on your ability to teach.

This guide walks you through exactly what to do after a **failed Praxis 5001** attempt: how to read your score report, how the **Praxis retake policy** works, what it costs, and how to change your approach so the next attempt is your last. Every policy rule below links to an official ETS page; the fees and dates are a snapshot, not a promise.

## Step 1: Read Your Score Report and Confirm Your State's Line

Before you register for a retake, understand *why* you failed. Your ETS score report shows your scaled score, your state's qualifying score, and—most importantly—**raw points earned versus raw points available in each content category.**

If you failed the 5002 with a 152 and your state requires 157, look at where those five points disappeared. Did "writing, speaking, and listening" drag you down? Did "reading comprehension" cost you? The ratio of earned to available points tells you exactly where to focus. (Example: a 152 vs. a 157 line is five scaled points apart — that is not five questions; scaled points and raw points are different things.)

**Praxis passing scores vary by state.** There is no universal number, and there is no generic "one score for the whole 5001." For example, South Carolina's current elementary lines are not a single four-subtest battery anymore: math 5003/5903 = 157 (or 8003 = 152), social studies 5004/5904 = 155 (or 8004 = 147), science 5005/5905 = 159 (or 8005 = 143), with a separate reading requirement (5205 = 159 or Pearson 890 = 233).[5] If you are in South Carolina, read the transition and historical-score rules on the [by-state page](https://learndiag.com/praxis-5001-passing-score-by-state) before choosing a retake code. If you scored 155 and your path requires 157, you need refinement; if you scored 138, you need foundational rebuilding.

Log into your ETS account, check your state's current requirements, and write down the exact qualifying score. Do not rely on outdated advice.

## The Praxis Retake Policy: Rules, Costs, and the 28-Day Wait

### You Only Retake What You Failed

The Praxis 5001 is a multiple-subtest exam. The four sections—5002, 5003, 5004, and 5005—are scored independently. Pass three and fail one? You only retake the failed subtest. Your passing scores on the other three remain valid.

### The 28-Day Waiting Period

ETS enforces a mandatory **28-day waiting period** between attempts on the same test or subtest. Took the 5003 on August 1st? Your earliest retake is 28 days later — in that example, August 29th. The wait applies to the *same test code* and is separate from booking availability: you can schedule a new appointment before the window ends, but the new test date must satisfy the waiting period. This applies even if you canceled your scores on the earlier attempt. Violate the rule, and ETS cancels the retest scores and keeps the fee.[1]

The restriction applies per test, not across different tests. If you failed 5003 but want to take a different Praxis test, you can — you just cannot retake 5003 until the window passes. Official expectations: see the [ETS retake and appointment page](https://praxis.ets.org/manage-praxis-test-appointment.html) before booking.

### What It Costs to Retake

Registering for a retake is a normal registration at the current price for that test code — there is no special "retake discount." As of the September 2026 check, the 5003 product page listed $64 and the combined 5001 listed $180; individual subtest prices are set per code and per registration date, so verify the current amount for *your* code on the official ETS Praxis fees page before registering.[2] Some states add surcharges or have their own payment steps.

You can also choose to retake failed subtests one at a time, spreading out both cost and preparation pressure.

### The "Free After Three" Safety Net

ETS runs **Free After Three**. The eligibility conditions, per the official page:[6]

- It applies to the **same test** — you cannot mix attempts across different test codes into one count.
- Your first attempt counted for the program must be on or after **October 1, 2025**.
- You must have taken the same test at least **three times, each with a reported score**.
- You then qualify for **free retakes for five years from the score report date of your third attempt**.
- Steps follow the corresponding Steps counts for your test.

The official page's main flow says the discount is applied automatically when you register; the FAQ still describes a per-attempt voucher request process with a six-month validity window for some cases. The program materials are not fully consistent with each other on that point — if the discount does not appear at registration, **contact ETS directly** about your case rather than assuming a specific email timeline. This guide does not invent a new process for you; the conditions above are what the official page states.

The program exists because ETS recognizes that some candidates need more than one or two attempts — and financial barriers should not end a teaching career.

## How to Use the Waiting Period: A Four-Week Template

Twenty-eight days is a built-in study window, not a punishment. Treat the plan below as a **template** — it is a review framework, not a promise that 28 days guarantees a pass, and hitting a self-set practice percentage is not an official passing standard.

**Week 1: Diagnose.** Pull your score report. List every content category where you earned less than 60% of available raw points. Gather resources aligned to those weak areas — not general review books.

**Week 2: Rebuild.** Focus on content acquisition. Work through example problems with detailed explanations. Close knowledge gaps before simulating test day. If you were at 45% in one category and 85% in another, the 45% category gets the time — but a two-set sample like that is not enough to declare a review priority by itself; note question counts, domain, and difficulty.

**Week 3: Practice Under Pressure.** Transition to timed practice. Impose real test-day limits: the 5002 gives you 90 minutes for 80 questions; the 5003 gives you 65 minutes for 50 questions; the 5004 gives 60/60 and the 5005's timing is 55 questions with a timing figure to confirm on the official page. Practice at that pace, with the question count and total time for each timed set written down.

**Week 4: Simulate and Decide.** Take a full-length, timed practice set. If your score is consistently above your state's threshold by a comfortable margin, register for the retake. If not, push the date back. There is no prize for retaking quickly — only for retaking once and passing.

## Retake Strategies That Actually Work

### 1. Study Your Score Report, Not the Whole Book

The most common mistake is restarting a 400-page study guide from page one. That wastes time on content you already know. Your score report's category breakdown is your syllabus. If you earned 85% in Earth and Space Science but only 45% in Physical Science, every minute on Earth and Space is stolen from Physical Science.

### 2. Switch Study Modalities

If you studied by reading a book and failed, switch to video + practice questions. If flashcards did not work, try full-length simulated exams. The brain encodes information differently through different channels. A modality switch often breaks through a plateau that repetition cannot.

### 3. Focus on One Subtest at a Time

If you failed multiple subtests, resist bundling them on the same day. It saves registration fees but dilutes preparation. Concentrate on one subtest, pass it, then move to the next. The confidence boost from clearing one failure is worth more than scheduling convenience.

### 4. Fix Pacing, Not Just Content

Many failures are timing failures, not knowledge failures. Candidates spend five minutes on one hard question, then rush ten easy ones. If you are missing questions in the final third of a section, your problem is the clock.

Learn the strategic skip. If a question will take more than 90 seconds, mark it and move on. A skipped question costs zero points. A rushed question costs the point *and* the time.

### 5. Use Practice Scores as Your Green Light

Feeling prepared is not reliable. Passing a practice test safely above your state's cut score is. Use an objective benchmark before rebooking. If your practice scores are not consistently above the passing line, you are not ready—regardless of study hours logged.

## Managing the Mental Game

Failing a high-stakes exam hits harder than people admit. You may worry about your student teaching placement, your graduation timeline, or whether you belong in this profession.

Those feelings are normal. They are also temporary.

**There is no limit on Praxis attempts.** You can retake as many times as you need. Schools care that you hold certification. They do not care how many attempts it took. Your score report does not display attempt history. A passing score on attempt four clears the exact same bar as a passing score on attempt one.

If you spiral into anxiety, talk to a teacher who failed a certification exam and came back. Their perspective normalizes your situation in a way generic encouragement cannot. And separate identity from performance. You are not "bad at math." You are "a candidate who has not yet passed the Praxis 5003." The second framing leaves room for growth.

## When to Consider a Different Path

Most candidates who change their approach do pass on a later attempt — but there is no published statistics table on pass-by-attempt here, so treat that as a general observation, not a promise. If you have failed the same subtest four or more times, ask honest questions: Are your materials aligned to the current test blueprint? Is there a foundational gap that weeks of review cannot close? Are test-day anxiety or language barriers affecting performance more than knowledge?

If the gap is foundational — say, you have not taken math since high school — consider a formal content course before attempting again. Also verify with your state agency whether an alternative exam or a provisional license could fit your situation; do not rely on a forum claim that "your state definitely offers X."

## Final Word: A Speed Bump, Not a Roadblock

A **failed Praxis 5001** subtest is frustrating and expensive. But it is not a referendum on your potential as a teacher. The Praxis tests content knowledge and test-taking skill. Teaching is a profession of relationship, judgment, and care. Many outstanding teachers failed a Praxis subtest on their first try.

Treat this failure as data. Read the score report. Confirm your state's exact requirement. Use the 28-day window strategically. Change your study approach—do not just study harder. Register when your practice scores say you are ready, not when your anxiety says you need to get it over with.

You have a path forward. It is well-defined, well-traveled, and open to you.
