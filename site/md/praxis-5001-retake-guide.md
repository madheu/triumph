# Failed a Praxis 5001 Subtest? Retake Rules, Costs, and What to Do Next

*2026-08-16 · Reading time: about 3 min*

You opened your Praxis score report and saw it: **NOT PASSED** on one—or maybe two—of your Elementary Education: Multiple Subjects subtests. Maybe it was Reading and Language Arts (5002). Maybe math (5003). Maybe Science (5005) got you by two points.

Here is the truth: **failing a Praxis 5001 subtest is common.** The 5001 is a broad four-subject battery covering reading pedagogy, mathematics, social studies, and science. Most elementary teachers have natural strength in two or three areas and gaps in the others. A subtest failure is a data point about preparation, not a verdict on your ability to teach.

This guide walks you through exactly what to do after a **failed Praxis 5001** attempt: how to read your score report, how the **Praxis retake policy** works, what it costs, and how to change your approach so the next attempt is your last.

## Step 1: Read Your Score Report and Confirm Your State's Line

Before you register for a retake, understand *why* you failed. Your ETS score report shows your scaled score, your state's qualifying score, and—most importantly—**raw points earned versus raw points available in each content category.**

If you failed the 5002 with a 152 and your state requires 157, look at where those five points disappeared. Did "writing, speaking, and listening" drag you down? Did "reading comprehension" cost you? The ratio of earned to available points tells you exactly where to focus.

**Praxis passing scores vary by state.** There is no universal number. South Carolina requires 157 on Reading/Language Arts, 157 on Math, 155 on Social Studies, and 159 on Science. Washington and Utah both require 157 on the full exam. If you scored 155 and your state requires 157, you need refinement. If you scored 138, you need foundational rebuilding.

Log into your ETS account, check your state's current requirements, and write down the exact qualifying score. Do not rely on outdated advice.

## The Praxis Retake Policy: Rules, Costs, and the 28-Day Wait

### You Only Retake What You Failed

The Praxis 5001 is a multiple-subtest exam. The four sections—5002, 5003, 5004, and 5005—are scored independently. Pass three and fail one? You only retake the failed subtest. Your passing scores on the other three remain valid.

### The 28-Day Waiting Period

ETS enforces a mandatory **28-day waiting period** between attempts on the same test or subtest. Took the 5003 on August 1st? Your earliest retake is August 29th. This applies even if you canceled your scores. Violate this rule, and ETS cancels your retest scores and keeps your fee.

The restriction applies per subtest, not per exam. If you failed 5003 but want to take a different Praxis test, you can. You just cannot retake 5003 until day 29.

### What It Costs to Retake

There is no retake discount. You pay full price for each subtest. Individual 5001 subtests cost approximately **$64** when taken separately, or **$180** for the full combined exam. Exact fees vary and are subject to change—some states add surcharges—so verify current amounts on the official ETS Praxis fees page before registering.

You can also choose to retake failed subtests one at a time, spreading out both cost and preparation pressure.

### The "Free After 3" Safety Net

Here is good news many candidates miss: ETS runs **Free After 3**. If your first attempt was on or after **October 1, 2025**, and you have taken the same test three times with reported scores, you qualify for **free retakes** for five years from your third attempt's score report date.

ETS emails you a voucher request form within three days of your third score report. Each voucher is valid for six months, and you can request unlimited free retakes within that five-year window. The program covers all Praxis test titles, including 5001 subtests, though it is not available in every state and does not cover Praxis Bridge products.

This policy exists because ETS recognizes that some candidates need more than one or two attempts—and financial barriers should not end a teaching career.

## How to Use the 28-Day Waiting Period

Twenty-eight days is a built-in study window, not a punishment. Use it strategically.

**Week 1: Diagnose.** Pull your score report. List every content category where you earned less than 60% of available raw points. Gather resources aligned to those weak areas—not general review books.

**Week 2: Rebuild.** Focus on content acquisition. Watch targeted video lessons. Work through example problems with detailed explanations. Close knowledge gaps before simulating test day.

**Week 3: Practice Under Pressure.** Transition to timed practice. Impose real test-day limits. The 5002 gives you 90 minutes for 80 questions. The 5003 gives you 65 minutes for 50 questions. Practice at that pace.

**Week 4: Simulate and Decide.** Take a full-length, timed practice test. If your score is consistently above your state's threshold by a comfortable margin, register for the retake. If not, push the date back. There is no prize for retaking quickly—only for retaking once and passing.

## Retake Strategies That Actually Work

### 1. Study Your Score Report, Not the Whole Book

The most common mistake is restarting a 400-page study guide from page one. That wastes time on content you already know. Your score report's category breakdown is your syllabus. If you earned 85% in Earth and Space Science but only 45% in Physical Science, every minute on Earth and Space is stolen from Physical Science.

### 2. Switch Study Modalities

If you studied by reading a book and failed, switch to video + practice questions. If flashcards did not work, try full-length simulated exams. The brain encodes information differently through different channels. A modality switch often breaks through a plateau that repetition cannot.

### 3. Focus on One Subtest at a Time

If you failed multiple subtests, resist bundling them on the same day. It saves registration fees but dilutes preparation. Concentrate on one subtest, pass it, then move to the next. The confidence boost from clearing one failure is worth more than scheduling convenience.

### 4. Fix Pacing, Not Just Content

Many failures are timing failures, not knowledge failures. Candidates spend five minutes on one hard question, then rush ten easy ones. If you are missing questions in the final third of a section, your problem is the clock.

Learn the strategic skip. If a question will take more than 90 seconds, mark it and move on. A skipped question costs zero points. A rushed question costs the point *and* the time.

### 5. Use Practice Scores as Your Green Light

Feeling prepared is not reliable. Passing a practice test safely above your state's cut score is. Use an objective benchmark before rebooking. If your practice scores are not consistently above the passing line, you are not ready—regardless of study hours logged.

## Managing the Mental Game

Failing a high-stakes exam hits harder than people admit. You may worry about your student teaching placement, your graduation timeline, or whether you belong in this profession.

Those feelings are normal. They are also temporary.

**There is no limit on Praxis attempts.** You can retake as many times as you need. Schools care that you hold certification. They do not care how many attempts it took. Your score report does not display attempt history. A passing score on attempt four clears the exact same bar as a passing score on attempt one.

If you spiral into anxiety, talk to a teacher who failed a certification exam and came back. Their perspective normalizes your situation in a way generic encouragement cannot. And separate identity from performance. You are not "bad at math." You are "a candidate who has not yet passed the Praxis 5003." The second framing leaves room for growth.

## When to Consider a Different Path

Most candidates pass on the second or third attempt with a better plan. But if you have failed the same subtest four or more times, ask honest questions: Are your materials aligned to the current test blueprint? Is there a foundational gap that weeks of review cannot close? Are test-day anxiety or language barriers affecting performance more than knowledge?

If the gap is foundational—say, you have not taken math since high school—consider a formal content course before attempting again. Also verify whether your state offers provisional licenses that let you teach while completing Praxis requirements. Some states have flexibility your program may not advertise.

## Final Word: A Speed Bump, Not a Roadblock

A **failed Praxis 5001** subtest is frustrating and expensive. But it is not a referendum on your potential as a teacher. The Praxis tests content knowledge and test-taking skill. Teaching is a profession of relationship, judgment, and care. Many outstanding teachers failed a Praxis subtest on their first try.

Treat this failure as data. Read the score report. Confirm your state's exact requirement. Use the 28-day window strategically. Change your study approach—do not just study harder. Register when your practice scores say you are ready, not when your anxiety says you need to get it over with.

You have a path forward. It is well-defined, well-traveled, and open to you.
